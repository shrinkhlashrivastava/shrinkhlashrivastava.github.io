Smart IVR: call flows that flag their own problems

Businesses on AngelPBX use Smart IVR to decide what callers hear and where each call goes. I audited the existing builder, explored a full redesign, scoped it with engineering, and handed off a Core IVR where every step shows whether it will work before a caller finds out.

Why Smart IVR?
It is the one module where I can show the whole loop: audit, market research, a broad concept, a hard scope decision with engineering, handoff, a redesign after handoff, and testing with live agents.

The product

One person builds the menu. Every caller lives with it.

IVR is the phone menu a caller hears: “Press 1 for Sales.” In AngelPBX, an admin builds that menu as a flow of steps: a greeting, a menu, and a destination for each key press.

Mistakes here are quiet. A greeting with no audio plays silence. A menu option with no destination is a dead end. The admin rarely hears it; the caller always does.

Merging is where the audit got useful

Twenty-eight issues is a list nobody prioritises. Many of them were the same problem seen from different screens. I merged overlaps and kept a log of every merge, so nothing was lost and every consolidated issue could be traced back to what I saw.

I rated each one Extreme, Major or Minor. These were my working ratings for prioritising, not measured user impact.

02 · What I found

Three problems shaped everything that followed

Most of the fifteen issues traced back to three things: setup was split across two places, the canvas controls said the wrong thing, and nothing told you when a flow was broken.

03 · Benchmark

Then I studied how the market solves the same job

I went through Vonage AI Studio and RingCentral’s RingCX IVR builder, using their product screens and documentation. I wasn’t looking for features to copy. I wanted the patterns both had settled on, because those are what admins would expect.

Configure a step where you build it

Both builders use a node palette beside the canvas. Selecting a node opens its settings in a panel, so you never leave the flow.

AngelPBX then: settings in a separate form, a cramped pop-up picker.
Vonage AI Studio: nodes dragged onto the canvas, settings open on the right. RingCX: palette of nodes, drag and connect.

Failure handling lives inside the input step

Retries, “no input” and “invalid input” paths belong to the step that asks the caller something, not to a global settings page.

AngelPBX then: “Confirm Attempts” and “Max Failure” as abstract fields, far from the menu they affect.
Vonage Collect Input: retries plus “No Input” and “Missed” paths. RingCX: Get Input node.

Check the flow before callers do

Both treat testing and publishing as first-class actions, and RingCX makes business hours a node in the flow.

AngelPBX then: every change went live on save, with no time-based routing.
Vonage: Tester, editor mode and publish. RingCX: testing call routing, Date Time node.

Competitor interfaces are described here rather than reproduced.

04 · Exploration Concept

First, I designed for where the product should go

Modern IVR answered almost every audit finding at once: a 21-node palette across voice, messaging and logic, a flow switcher, draft and publish states, undo, templates, version history and a call simulator. My design manager and a senior designer reviewed the direction with me.

05 · Scope

Then engineering sized it, and we phased the work

In the feasibility review, the development lead sized the full Modern scope as more than the team could build in the first release. We agreed to phase it: Core IVR’s calling workflows first, with the advanced capabilities parked for a later add-on phase.

I cut it down twice, first to a slimmer second version, then to the Core IVR that went to development. The question each time was the same: what does an admin need to build a calling flow they can trust?

First release Core IVR

  • Nine calling nodes with one-line descriptions
  • Settings in a side panel, next to the flow
  • Node health on every step
  • Undo, redo, auto layout and version history
  • Timings in seconds, with valid ranges and helper text
  • Audio upload or text-to-speech, with preview

Add-on phase Modern capabilities

  • AI assistant and speech-recognition nodes
  • Messaging channels: SMS, WhatsApp, rich cards
  • Logic and integrations: conditions, webhooks, flow jumps
  • Templates and reuse
  • Flow analytics and KPIs

06 · Core IVR Handed off

One place to set up, build and configure a flow

Core IVR keeps the parts of the exploration that fix the audit’s biggest problems, inside a scope the team could build.

Set up

A drawer that explains itself

Creating an IVR now happens in one drawer over the list. Each field says what it does and what values it accepts.

Flow type explains master and child flows. Timeouts are in seconds. Max digits goes up to 20, so a phone number fits. The menu audio can be uploaded or typed as text-to-speech, with supported formats shown up front.

Build

A toolbar with a way back

Undo and redo, zoom, auto layout, version history and publish sit in one toolbar. Version history records who changed what, with a summary of nodes and connections added or removed.

Configure

Settings open beside the flow, not instead of it

Selecting a node opens its settings in a panel on the right, so the flow stays visible. For handoff I specified that the panel’s header and footer stay fixed while the middle scrolls, so Save and Exit are always in reach.

Menu node

Each option shows where it goes

Menu options are digit and label pairs you can reorder by dragging. Once an option is wired up on the canvas, the panel shows its destination, so an admin can check routing from either side.

The panel’s health check names the exact options that still lead nowhere.

Node states

Actions appear when you need them

On the canvas, settings and delete appear only when you hover over a node. That replaced the always-visible X and pencil the audit flagged as misleading. Connected nodes show their link clearly, and the board below went to developers as the spec for every state.

07 · Node health

Show what’s broken before a caller hears it

The audit’s most dangerous gap was silent failure: invalid steps saved without a word. I designed a health check that runs on every node, explains the problem in plain words, and tells the admin what the caller would experience.

The idea started in Modern IVR and was one of the things that made it through the scope cut into Core. I designed the states and wrote the rules for each node type.

Healthy

Configured and connected to the next step.

Warning

Works, but something will cause trouble later, like a missing label or an unconnected option.

Error

The caller will hit a problem: silence, a dead end, or a call that can’t be transferred.

Rules written for each node type

A missing label is a warning everywhere. What counts as an error depends on what the node does for the caller.

08 · After handoff

The audio picker, redesigned around a new library

After handoff, the team added a shared library of ready-made audio for customers. I redesigned how greetings are chosen around it: browse by name, hear it before you pick it, or add your own by upload or text-to-speech.

09 · Delivery

I stayed with it from handoff to live calls

The design took 7–10 days from audit to handoff. My involvement continued through the build.

Handoff

The whole flow, not loose screens

I handed off every screen, node state and panel, with behaviour notes in the file, like the fixed panel header and footer and the named toolbar controls. Then I walked the developers through the complete flow.

Build review

Daily review of the build

I reviewed the developed module and the audio flow every day, alongside QA and the team, and logged the functional and visual issues I found for developers to fix.

Live testing

Tested with real agents

The module was tested in a working call centre run by the company’s founders, with live agents taking real calls through the flows.

Reflection

Next time, I’d bring engineering into scoping before polishing the full concept.

The broad exploration was useful. It gave the team a roadmap and produced ideas like node health that survived the cut. But sizing it with developers a few days earlier would have saved a design round.

I’d also agree on success measures before handoff, such as time to build a first flow, or how many flows go live with errors, so the impact can be measured and not only seen.
